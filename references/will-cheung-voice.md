# Will Cheung voice profile

Apply this optional profile only when the user explicitly asks for Will Cheung's voice or identifies themself as the writer of these samples. For anyone else, infer voice from their material and use the general guide.

This profile captures recurring language decisions, not article structure. Do not reproduce the samples, their subject matter, or their errors.

## Core stance

- Write as a curious builder reporting what actually happened.
- Lead with personal reaction, expectation, or limitation when the user supplied one.
- Sound technically informed without pretending to be a formal engineer or detached analyst.
- Let enthusiasm show, then test it against a concrete failure, caveat, or awkward detail.
- Prefer a clear personal judgment over a neutral product-summary voice.

## Small word choices

Use ordinary modifiers when they fit the thought: “a bit,” “pretty,” “mostly,” “actually,” “just,” “even,” “somewhat,” “kind of,” “maybe,” and “right away.” They soften or sharpen a real judgment; do not sprinkle them in mechanically.

Favor familiar verbs: build, try, test, use, fix, ship, get stuck, wrestle with, figure out, go live, and work. Prefer concrete verbs over “leverage,” “facilitate,” “enable,” or “optimize.”

Refer back casually with phrases such as “that part,” “this one,” “the whole thing,” “at first,” “so far,” and “after the fact” when the reference is obvious.

Use contractions. Prefer “doesn't,” “it's,” “I'd,” and “can't” unless emphasis requires the full form.

## Sentence decisions

- Mix a long, slightly wandering setup with a short reaction or verdict.
- Start with “And,” “But,” or “So” when it continues the thought naturally.
- Use a fragment after a complete sentence when the fragment delivers an honest reaction. One is enough.
- Allow an occasional parenthetical for a caveat, correction, or self-aware joke.
- Use a rhetorical question when it is a real decision point, not as a manufactured hook.
- Repeat a key word when a person would repeat it aloud. Do not replace every repetition with a synonym.
- Keep minor looseness that carries spoken rhythm, but do not manufacture grammatical mistakes.

## Humor and comparisons

The humor usually undercuts the writer, the tool, or an overconfident result. It is rarely a polished standalone joke.

Good moves include:

- Admit limited credit after describing something impressive.
- Follow a product promise with the detail that did not work.
- Compare an AI tool to a coworker, intern, student, familiar gadget, or everyday frustration.
- Put the deflating detail at the end of the sentence or in a short parenthetical.

Use one specific joke where the material supports it. Do not add sarcasm to every paragraph or invent a personal failure for comic effect.

## Enthusiasm without marketing copy

Will sometimes uses strong language: amazing, magical, mind-blowing, insanely good, brutal, terrible, or pure magic. Preserve that energy when it comes from a reported test or reaction. Pair praise with what specifically worked. Pair criticism with what got stuck or failed.

Avoid replacing a blunt judgment with corporate balance. “It worked right away, but collaboration didn't” is closer than a polished paragraph about strengths and limitations.

Keep uncertainty explicit with language such as “I think,” “I believe,” “I can see,” “I didn't expect,” or “I do wish” when the source is genuinely uncertain.

## What to preserve from a ramble

Prioritize:

1. Concrete numbers, elapsed time, tools, prompts, and outcomes
2. The expectation before the test and the reaction afterward
3. Small admissions, reversals, and caveats
4. Phrases that sound like something the writer would say aloud
5. Strong opinions that the user actually expressed

If one of these is missing and the piece depends on it, ask. Do not invent a more colorful anecdote, stronger opinion, or cleaner lesson.

## Avoid caricature

- Do not force a confession into every opening.
- Do not turn every observation into setup, punchline, lesson.
- Do not copy favorite metaphors or phrases from the samples.
- Do not overuse exclamation points, emojis, parentheses, or rhetorical questions.
- Do not preserve a typo or grammar error merely because it appeared in an older article.
- Do not make the writer sound more certain, technical, cynical, or excited than the source supports.
